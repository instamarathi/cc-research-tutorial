# Tips for Writing with CC

## 1. Write section by section — review and approve before moving on

CC generates best when you give it a short target and a clear mandate. Drafting one subsection, reading it, then proceeding keeps you in the steering seat. When you ask for a whole section at once, you can't correct a wrong turn until it has propagated through everything that follows. A paragraph-level review loop costs ten minutes; untangling a structurally flawed five-page section costs hours.

## 2. Give CC the section's job before asking it to write

Don't say "write the Methods section." Say: "The Methods section needs to (1) justify the sample size, (2) describe the instrument and its validation history, and (3) explain the analysis pipeline in replicable detail." The explicit job description shapes every sentence CC produces. Without it, CC will write a plausible Methods section for some paper — not necessarily yours.

## 3. Ask CC to check argument flow, not just grammar

CC can evaluate the logical architecture of your writing, not only the surface quality. Prompt: "Does the Discussion build logically from the Results? Identify any place where the Discussion makes a claim that isn't directly grounded in a result you reported." This is a higher-value check than grammar or word choice and harder to do manually when you're too close to the material.

## 4. Draft the hardest parts first — Introduction and Conclusion

These are the hardest sections to write because they require knowing what the whole paper does. But they're also the sections that shape everything else. Draft them early, even roughly. Having them pinned down — the question you're answering, the contribution you're claiming — makes every subsequent section easier to write because you know exactly what it's serving. CC can hold these anchors in context and check every middle section against them.

## 5. Tell CC the voice convention explicitly

Academic writing varies substantially across disciplines. Don't assume CC will match your field's conventions by default. Specify: "Write in passive voice, past tense, as is standard in quantitative sociology." Or: "Write in active voice, present tense, following conventions for theoretical computer science." If your target journal has specific style requirements, state those too. The more precisely you specify the register, the less editing you'll need to do on tone after the fact.

## 6. Ask CC to flag hedging and overclaiming

Every academic draft has two failure modes: sentences that claim more than the data supports, and sentences that hedge so aggressively they say nothing. Ask CC: "Read this section and mark any sentences that overclaim beyond what the data or analysis can support. Also mark any sentences that are unnecessarily hedged to the point of being uninformative." Review both sets. Overclaims are reviewer magnets; unnecessary hedges weaken your contribution. This single pass often produces the most editorial value of any CC review step.
