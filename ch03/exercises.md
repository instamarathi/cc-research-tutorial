# Exercises: Developing Theory

## Exercise 1: Formalize an Intuition

**Time: 15 minutes**

Take a research idea you're currently developing — or one you've been thinking about but haven't written down. It doesn't need to be polished.

**Step 1.** Write 3-5 sentences describing the idea in plain language: what the phenomenon is, what you think causes it, and under what conditions the relationship should hold.

**Step 2.** Give the paragraph to CC with this prompt:

> "Help me turn this into a precise, testable hypothesis. Identify the key variables, the proposed relationship between them, and the boundary conditions under which the hypothesis should hold."

**Step 3.** Ask CC to formalize it further:

> "Express this hypothesis in LaTeX using appropriate mathematical notation."

**Step 4.** Ask CC for reviewer pressure:

> "What are the two strongest objections a reviewer in my field would raise against this hypothesis?"

Compare CC's formal version against your original intuition. Notice where they diverge — those divergences are usually places where your thinking was still underspecified.

## Exercise 2: The Steelman Drill

**Time: 15 minutes**

Pick a paper whose central claim you find unconvincing. It should be a paper you've read, not just heard about — you need to know the argument in some detail.

**Step 1.** Write out the paper's key claim in 2-3 sentences.

**Step 2.** Give it to CC:

> "Steelman this claim. Give me the most rigorous, defensible version of the argument — fill in implicit assumptions, identify the strongest supporting evidence, and sharpen the logical structure."

**Step 3.** Read CC's steelmanned version. If it's stronger than the original paper's presentation, note how. Then ask:

> "What is the single weakest assumption in the steelmanned version? Which assumption, if false, would most directly undermine the entire argument?"

The goal is to understand what would have to be true for the claim to hold — and to decide whether you believe those things. This is also a useful preparation technique before writing a critique or lit review section.

## Exercise 3: Build a Logical Chain

**Time: 10 minutes**

Take the central thesis of a paper you're writing or planning to write. State it in a single sentence.

**Step 1.** Give the thesis to CC:

> "Break down the logical chain from foundational premises to this conclusion. Number each inferential step and make every link explicit — do not skip steps."

**Step 2.** Review the chain. Look for steps where the connection between consecutive items feels like a leap rather than a derivation.

**Step 3.** Ask CC:

> "Which step in this logical chain relies most heavily on unestablished empirical assumptions? Which step is most vulnerable to challenge by a skeptical reviewer?"

Use CC's answer to decide where your paper needs more anticipatory argumentation, more empirical support, or an explicit acknowledgment of a limiting assumption.
