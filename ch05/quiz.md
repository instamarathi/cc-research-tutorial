## Q1

Why does AI-generated academic writing often feel generic, even when it is factually correct and grammatically clean?

- [ ] AI models have limited vocabulary and tend to repeat the same words
- [ ] AI cannot understand academic conventions or disciplinary norms
- [x] LLMs average across millions of texts and produce statistically central prose — smooth and correct, but with no individual voice or rhythm
- [ ] AI writing is generic because it lacks access to recent research

> A large language model learns from hundreds of millions of texts and produces writing that is statistically central to all of them — offending no one, sounding like no one. The result is technically fine prose that carries no individual fingerprint. That is why AI writing is so recognizable: it sounds like the average of everything, rather than like a particular person thinking through a particular problem.

You have created `style-guide.md` from your own writing samples. How do you use it at the start of a writing session?

- [ ] Open the file and re-read it before writing prompts yourself, then close it
- [ ] Ask CC to rewrite the style guide in simpler language each session so it is fresh
- [ ] Append the full contents of the style guide to every prompt you send
- [x] Begin the session with "Read style-guide.md. Write in the style described there." — one prompt that orients CC for the whole session

> CC holds the style guide in its context for the entire session once you point it to the file. You do not need to paste it into every prompt or re-read it yourself. A single orienting prompt at the start — "Read style-guide.md. Write in the style described there." — is enough to anchor all subsequent writing tasks in that session.

## Q3

What makes the style guide approach ethically sound as a form of academic authorship?

- [ ] It is ethical because CC discloses in the text that it was involved in writing
- [ ] It is ethical only if the final draft is reviewed by a supervisor
- [ ] Style guides are not yet considered ethically established — their status is still debated
- [x] The guide is extracted from the student's own writing — CC writes in their voice, guided by their own examples, so the intellectual and stylistic choices remain theirs

> Authorship in academic writing is about whose intellectual and stylistic choices shaped the work — not about who physically typed the sentences. When the style guide comes from your own prose, and the content (arguments, evidence, claims) comes from your own thinking, CC is executing your choices, not replacing them. The resulting prose is genuinely yours. CC is the typist.

**Feature 2 — Hedging language:** "I hedge with 'appears to' and 'the data suggest' rather than 'shows' or 'proves.' Other valid examples: a characteristic way of introducing citations ("As Smith (2018) argues…" vs. "Smith (2018) has shown…"), a preference for short Anglo-Saxon words over Latinate vocabulary, a habit of opening paragraphs with a question, or a tendency to close paragraphs with a forward-looking sentence that links to the next point. The key is being specific enough that you could actually test CC's output against the criterion.
